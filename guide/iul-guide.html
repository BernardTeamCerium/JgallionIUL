Welcome

Clear Answers, No Pressure.

Indexed Universal Life is one of the most talked-about, and most misunderstood, tools in retirement planning.

Some people hear it described as a way to get market gains with no risk. Others hear it is always a bad deal. The truth sits in between: an IUL is a life insurance policy with real benefits and real trade-offs, and whether it fits depends on your goals, your budget and how the policy is designed.

This guide walks through the seven things I believe everyone should understand before using an IUL as part of a retirement strategy: what it may provide, and where its limits are. Read it at your own pace, write down your questions, and use the checklist near the end when you talk with any insurance professional, including me.

The Basics

The basics

An IUL in Plain Language

An Indexed Universal Life (IUL) policy is permanent life insurance. It pays a death benefit to your beneficiaries and can build cash value over time, with interest credited based on the performance of a market index.

You pay premiums into the policy. The insurer deducts the cost of insurance and other charges. What remains becomes cash value, which earns interest through a fixed account, one or more index accounts, or both. Over time, that cash value may be available to you through policy loans or withdrawals, while the death benefit protects the people who depend on you.

1. PremiumsYou pay in, within limits set by the policy and tax law. Many IULs offer flexible premiums.
→
2. ChargesCost of insurance, policy fees and any rider charges are deducted, usually every month.
→
3. Cash valueEarns interest linked to an index, subject to caps, participation rates and a floor.

The death benefit stays in place as long as the policy remains in force. Cash value may be accessed through loans or withdrawals, which reduce the death benefit.

What an IUL is

  • Permanent life insurance with a death benefit
  • A policy with cash value that may grow over time
  • Interest credited based on an index, with a floor against index losses
  • Flexible: premiums and death benefit can often be adjusted
  • A long-term commitment that needs ongoing reviews

What an IUL is not

  • An investment in the stock market or an index fund
  • A replacement for an emergency fund
  • A short-term savings account
  • “Market gains with no risk”: upside is limited and charges continue
  • A one-size-fits-all solution

7 Things to Understand

7 things to understand

Start With the Foundation

01

It’s Life Insurance First

An IUL is permanent life insurance with a death benefit. It is not a stock-market investment, and it should start with a real need for protection: a family that depends on your income, a mortgage, a business partner, or a legacy you want to leave.

Because it is insurance, you will go through underwriting. Your age, health and lifestyle affect the cost of coverage, and that cost is built into every year of the policy. If you don’t need life insurance, an IUL is rarely the most efficient place for your savings.

Ask your professionalHow much death benefit do I actually need, and how does that amount affect the cost and cash value of this policy?
02

How Index Crediting Works

Your cash value isn’t invested in the market directly. Instead, the insurer credits interest based on the performance of an index, such as the S&P 500®, over a set period called a crediting period or segment, often one year.

At the end of each period, the insurer looks at how the index moved, applies the policy’s cap, participation rate and floor, and credits interest to your cash value. Dividends paid by companies in the index are generally not included in the index return used for crediting.

Most policies offer several crediting strategies, including a fixed account, and let you choose how your cash value is allocated among them.

Ask your professionalWhich index strategies does this policy offer, how long is each crediting period, and how often can I change my allocation?

7 Things to Understand
03

Caps and Participation Rates

Upside is limited. A cap is the maximum interest rate that can be credited in a period. A participation rate is the share of the index gain used to calculate your interest. Some strategies also use a spread, a percentage subtracted from the index gain.

The insurer can usually change caps, participation rates and spreads over time, within the minimums guaranteed in the contract. A rate that looks attractive today may not stay that way.

Ask your professionalWhat are the current caps and participation rates, what are the guaranteed minimums, and how have this carrier’s rates changed in past years?
04

Floors Protect Against Index Losses

Many policies have a 0% floor, so a down year for the index isn’t credited as a loss to your cash value. That protection is a key reason people consider an IUL. But a floor applies to index crediting only: policy charges are still deducted in those years, so your cash value can still go down.

05

Policy Charges Matter

Cost of insurance, premium charges, administrative fees and rider charges all reduce cash value, especially in the early years. The cost of insurance generally rises as you get older, so how the policy is funded over time makes a big difference.

Most IULs also have surrender charges for the first 10 to 15 years. If you cancel the policy or take out too much during that time, you may get back less than you paid in.

Ask your professionalCan you walk me through every charge in this illustration, and what would I receive if I surrendered the policy in year 5 or year 10?
06

Loans Give Access, With Trade-offs

Once cash value has built up, you can borrow against it, often without credit checks and with flexible repayment. Under current tax law, policy loans are generally not taxable while the policy stays in force and is not a modified endowment contract (MEC).

But loans accrue interest, and any unpaid loan balance reduces the death benefit and the cash value available. If loans and charges grow larger than the cash value, the policy can lapse, and a lapse with an outstanding loan may create a taxable gain. Loan interest rates and loan types (fixed, variable or indexed) differ by carrier.

Ask your professionalWhat loan options does this policy offer, what are the rates, and how would we monitor loans so the policy doesn’t lapse?

7 Things to Understand
07

Many Values Aren’t Guaranteed

An IUL illustration shows how a policy might perform under certain assumptions. It usually includes a guaranteed column, based on maximum charges and minimum crediting, and a non-guaranteed column, based on current charges and an assumed interest rate.

Illustrations are hypothetical. Actual results depend on how the policy is funded, how the index performs, and changes to caps and charges. Comparing the guaranteed and non-guaranteed columns, and asking to see illustrations at lower assumed rates, gives a more realistic picture.

Ask your professionalCan I see this illustration at a lower assumed rate, and what happens in the guaranteed column?

Funding and Reviews: The Part People Skip

How you fund an IUL can matter as much as which policy you choose. Many people who use an IUL for cash value accumulation aim to fund it above the minimum premium, while staying within IRS limits so the policy isn’t classified as a modified endowment contract (MEC). A MEC loses some of the tax advantages of life insurance on loans and withdrawals.

An IUL is not “set it and forget it.” A yearly review of your in-force illustration, crediting rates, charges and any loans helps keep the policy on track with your goals.

Signs of a well-designed policy

  • Starts with a clear protection need
  • Premium you can afford for the long term
  • Illustrated at a reasonable, conservative rate
  • Funding stays within MEC limits

Red flags to watch for

  • Promises of “market returns with no risk”
  • Pressure to decide quickly
  • Only the most optimistic illustration shown
  • Funding with money you may need soon

The Bigger Picture

The bigger picture

The Third Bucket and Tax Diversification

Retirement planning isn’t only about how much you save. It’s also about where your money sits, and how it may be taxed when you need income.

Bucket 1Your 401(k)Tax-deferred growth and often an employer match. Withdrawals in retirement are generally taxed as ordinary income.
Bucket 2Your SavingsLiquid and accessible for emergencies and near-term goals, with growth that’s generally taxed along the way.
Bucket 3An Insurance StrategyProperly structured permanent life insurance adds a death benefit plus cash value that may be accessed later.

Tax now, tax later, or tax-advantaged. Savings and brokerage accounts are generally taxed as you go. Traditional 401(k)s and IRAs are generally taxed when you withdraw. Roth accounts and properly structured life insurance may offer tax-advantaged access. Having money in more than one tax category can give you more choices about where retirement income comes from in a given year.

An IUL’s death benefit is generally income-tax-free to beneficiaries under current law. Tax treatment of loans and withdrawals depends on the policy remaining in force and not becoming a MEC. Is It a Fit?

Is it a fit?

Who an IUL May Suit

An IUL tends to make the most sense when protection and long-term planning come together, and when the budget supports consistent funding for many years.

It may be worth exploring if you…

  • Need permanent life insurance for family, a mortgage or a legacy
  • Already contribute consistently to a 401(k) or maxed it out
  • Have an emergency fund and stable cash flow
  • Can fund a policy for 10 years or more
  • Want more tax diversification in retirement
  • Own a business and want assets outside the company
  • Are a high earner limited by retirement plan contribution caps

It may not be the right fit if you…

  • Don’t need life insurance
  • Have high-interest debt or no emergency savings
  • Aren’t yet capturing a full employer 401(k) match
  • May need the money within the next several years
  • Are uncomfortable with ongoing policy reviews
  • Only want the lowest-cost death benefit (term may fit better)

Who we often work with

Families planning for long-term protection, maxed-out 401(k) savers, business owners, and healthcare professionals balancing demanding careers with family. Every situation is different, which is why a conversation starts with your goals, not a product.

Your Checklist

Bring this to your review

Questions to Ask Before You Decide

Purpose

  • What problem is this policy solving for me: protection, accumulation, legacy, or all three?
  • Why an IUL instead of term life, whole life or simply investing more in my retirement accounts?

Design

  • How much death benefit is included, and why that amount?
  • What premium is planned, for how many years, and what happens if I pay less?
  • Is the funding designed to stay within MEC limits?
  • Which riders are included, and what does each one cost?

Crediting and costs

  • What are the current and guaranteed caps, participation rates and floors?
  • What are all the charges, and how do they change as I age?
  • What are the surrender charges, and for how many years?

Illustrations and access

  • Can I see the illustration at a lower assumed rate, and the guaranteed column?
  • How would loans or withdrawals work, and what are the loan rates?
  • How will we review this policy each year, and who will I work with?

Glossary

Reference

Key Terms, Simply Explained

Cash valueThe portion of the policy that can build over time after charges, and that may be accessed through loans or withdrawals.
Cap rateThe maximum interest rate that can be credited for a crediting period.
Cost of insurance (COI)The charge for the death benefit protection, based on your age, health and the amount at risk. It generally rises with age.
Crediting periodThe length of time, often one year, over which index performance is measured to calculate interest.
Death benefitThe amount paid to your beneficiaries when you pass away, reduced by any outstanding loans or withdrawals.
FloorThe minimum interest rate credited in a period, often 0%, so index losses aren’t credited to your cash value.
IllustrationA hypothetical projection of how a policy might perform under stated assumptions. It is not a guarantee.
IndexA measure of market performance, such as the S&P 500®, used to calculate interest. You are not invested in the index.
LapseWhen a policy ends because cash value can no longer cover charges and loan interest. Coverage stops and taxes may be due.
Modified endowment contract (MEC)A policy funded beyond IRS limits. Loans and withdrawals from a MEC lose some of life insurance’s tax advantages.
Participation rateThe percentage of the index gain used to calculate interest.
Policy loanMoney borrowed from the insurer using your cash value as collateral. Loans accrue interest and reduce the death benefit.
RiderAn optional add-on that changes or adds benefits, such as chronic or terminal illness benefits, usually for an extra charge.
SpreadA percentage subtracted from the index gain before interest is credited, used by some strategies instead of or alongside a cap.
Surrender chargeA fee for cancelling the policy or withdrawing large amounts during the early years.
UnderwritingThe process an insurer uses to review your health and other factors to approve coverage and set its cost.
